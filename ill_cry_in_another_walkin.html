I'll Cry in Another Walk-In

A Time Series Analysis of Quit Rates in Accommodation and Food Services

Jason Staats

Updated September 2026; data through July 2026

Overview

The accommodation and food services sector is widely associated with elevated employee turnover. This report examines monthly quit rates from January 2016 through July 2026 using the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey.

The central question is whether the post-pandemic surge in voluntary separations has fully unwound, or whether elevated turnover remains a structural feature of the industry. The July update adds another low reading to the recent series, strengthening the view that the extraordinary 2021-2022 quits surge has largely subsided at this level of aggregation.

ProviderU.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS)
PeriodJanuary 2016 - July 2026
FrequencyMonthly
UnitsQuits rate, percentage of employment

The JOLTS quits rate measures voluntary separations as a percentage of employment in the reference period. It captures worker-initiated exits and is distinct from layoffs, discharges, retirements, or other employer-initiated separations.

JOLTS data is based on a survey of business establishments and is subject to monthly revision as additional responses are received.

Methodology

The analysis follows a structured time-series workflow: full series visualization with a linear trend overlay, seasonality analysis, autocorrelation analysis, STL decomposition, and multi-model forecasting with holdout validation.

Quit-rate values from February 2020 through October 2020 were replaced with linearly interpolated estimates during decomposition and forecasting. This treats the early pandemic decline in quit activity as an external mobility shock rather than a durable change in the underlying quits-rate process.

Four forecasting approaches were trained on January 2016 through December 2024 and validated against January through December 2025. Model performance was assessed using Mean Absolute Error in percentage points. Several nonseasonal ARIMA specifications were compared within the ARIMA approach.

Full Series

Quit rates in accommodation and food services held relatively steady between 2016 and early 2020, averaging around 4.5%. A sharp but brief decline followed in early 2020, with rates recovering within a few months.

From mid-2021 through early 2022, quit rates climbed to a sustained peak of roughly 6%, well above anything seen in the pre-pandemic period. Rates have trended back down since, with dips below pre-pandemic levels appearing by late 2024. Volatility increased considerably in 2025, with the quits rate moving across a range of more than two percentage points.

Through July 2026, the swings have moderated, although rates have generally remained toward the lower end of the recent range. July 2026 recorded 3.5%, one of the lowest quit rates in the full dataset. The linear trend line sits near 4.7% across the full decade, suggesting stability at the aggregate level while masking the dramatic swings beneath it.

The decline from the pandemic-era peak and return to rates comparable with the pre-pandemic period suggest that much of the extraordinary increase has subsided, with little evidence so far of a fundamentally different structural baseline. From 2025 through July 2026, observed rates span 3.3% to 5.5%, compared with a pre-pandemic range of 3.9% to 5.1%. That overlap makes it difficult to distinguish meaningful structural change from ordinary variation at this level of precision.

COVID-19 Interpolation

Quit rates in early 2020 deviated sharply from the levels and patterns observed throughout the rest of the series. Because the goal of the subsequent analysis is to identify the underlying trend, seasonality, and persistence of the series, this unusual period could disproportionately influence those patterns.

To reduce that influence, quit-rate values from February through October 2020 were replaced using linear interpolation between the surrounding observations. This creates a smooth transition through the pandemic disruption rather than allowing those nine months to drive the characteristics identified by the models.

The original series is shown alongside the adjusted version above for transparency, and all subsequent analysis is based on the interpolated data.

Seasonality

Unlike employment in the sector, quits rates do not show a strong recurring seasonal pattern. Monthly averages are relatively flat, and the year-over-year overlay is driven more by level shifts over time than by a stable calendar rhythm.

The STL seasonal component reinforces this point. Seasonal movements are small relative to the broader changes in the series and do not show a strong, consistent annual pattern. Taken together, these results provide little evidence that a seasonal component is necessary for the forecasting models evaluated later.

Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation function shows how similar the series is to itself at different time lags, or how much current quit rates relate to past values.

The pattern here is a gradual, steady decline from lag 1 onward, with correlations remaining positive through lag 22 before crossing zero at lag 23. This indicates substantial persistence in the series, meaning that periods of relatively high or low quit rates tend to carry forward into subsequent months. Because the ACF is calculated on the level series, however, the gradual decline may reflect both serial dependence and longer-term movement rather than a specific autoregressive structure.

What is notably absent is the kind of discrete spike at lag 12 that would indicate strong annual seasonality. In a series with clear seasonal structure, you would expect to see a pronounced jump in correlation at the 12-month mark, reflecting the tendency to repeat the same pattern year over year. The ACF here does not show that, which is consistent with what the seasonality analysis found.

STL Decomposition

STL decomposition separates the adjusted series into observed, trend, seasonal, and residual components. The trend captures the longer arc of the data: relative stability through the pre-pandemic period, a smooth transition through the interpolated window, a clear climb to a peak around 2021 and 2022, and a gradual decline back toward earlier levels from 2023 onward.

The seasonal component is muted. Seasonal movements remain small relative to the broader changes in the series and do not show a strong or consistent annual pattern, reinforcing the findings from the earlier seasonality analysis.

The residual component captures what is left after trend and seasonality are removed. Most values stay close to zero, although deviations become more pronounced during periods of greater month-to-month movement, particularly in 2025.

The observed panel reflects the interpolated series. The smooth transition through early 2020 is a result of the interpolation rather than actual recorded quit activity.

Forecasting

To evaluate out-of-sample performance, the models forecast January through December 2025 after training on January 2016 through December 2024. Several nonseasonal ARIMA specifications were compared, and ARIMA(0,1,1) produced the lowest validation error.

ModelValidation MAE
ETS1.037 ppts
ARIMA(0,1,1)0.637 ppts
Naive0.725 ppts
OLS1.045 ppts

ARIMA(0,1,1) produced the lowest average error at 0.637 percentage points per month. The Naive model, which simply carries forward the last observed value, finished second and outperformed ETS. That result is consistent with the autocorrelation analysis, which showed substantial persistence and limited evidence of annual seasonality. With little seasonal structure to exploit, a simple persistence forecast is a tough benchmark to beat.

No model was positioned to anticipate the movements that occurred in 2025. Quit rates rose to 5.5% in June before falling sharply later in the year. While values at that level had appeared earlier in the series, the timing and magnitude of the 2025 swings were difficult to predict from prior observations.

ARIMA(0,1,1) was retrained on the full series through December 2025 and used to generate a 2026 forward forecast with 80% and 95% forecast intervals. The point forecast remains relatively flat, reflecting the same persistence behavior that made the model competitive in validation.

Actual quit rates through July 2026 have generally tracked below the point forecast while remaining within the broader range of plausible outcomes. The most recent observation, 3.5% in July, falls outside the narrower 80% interval but remains within the 95% interval. The wider interval exists to capture these less probable but still possible outcomes. The model continues to capture the general level of the series, although recent observations show more month-to-month variability than the point forecast suggests.

Key Findings

  • Quit rates averaged approximately 4.5% from 2016 through early 2020, reflecting an already elevated turnover baseline.
  • Quit rates rose sharply between mid-2021 and early 2022, reaching a sustained peak near 6%.
  • Rates declined from 2022 through 2024, with recent observations overlapping the pre-pandemic range.
  • The series has limited seasonal structure, in contrast to the strong summer employment peaks seen in the companion employment analysis.
  • ARIMA(0,1,1) performed best in validation with a Mean Absolute Error of 0.637 percentage points.
  • January through July 2026 quit rates ranged from 3.5% to 4.7%; July fell outside the 80% forecast interval but remained within the 95% interval.
