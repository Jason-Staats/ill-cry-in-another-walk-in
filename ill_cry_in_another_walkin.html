I'll Cry in Another Walk-In

A Time Series Analysis of Quit Rates in Accommodation and Food Services

Jason Staats

Updated November 2026 | Data through September 2026

Overview

This report examines monthly quit rates in accommodation and food services from January 2016 through September 2026 using seasonally adjusted BLS data. It is a companion to Up or On the Rocks?.

September brought a sharp rebound after the recent decline. The analysis examines that movement in historical context and evaluates a fixed 2026 forecast.

Methodology

The full-series chart uses original BLS observations. Subsequent analysis uses the series with February–October 2020 interpolated between January and November 2020. Models were trained through December 2024 and compared using 2025 validation MAE. The selected ARIMA model was then refitted through December 2025 for a fixed January–December 2026 forecast, without incorporating 2026 observations.

Full Series

Much like Up or On the Rocks, it's worth clarifying what this series is actually measuring. The JOLTS quits rate counts voluntary separations during the month as a percentage of employment. A worker who chooses to leave is a quit. Someone who gets laid off is not. That distinction matters because voluntary departures can offer insight into workers' willingness to leave their jobs without directly measuring their reasons for doing so.

This series covers accommodation and food services and is published by BLS on a seasonally adjusted basis. BLS makes that adjustment before the data is retrieved. No additional seasonal adjustment is applied in this notebook.

Quit rates were relatively steady between 2016 and 2019 compared with the larger movements that followed. A sharp decline occurred in early 2020, followed by a recovery toward pre-pandemic levels later that year.

From mid-2021 through early 2022, quit rates remained elevated, fluctuating around 6% and staying well above the pre-pandemic range. Rates subsequently trended downward, with observations below the pre-pandemic range appearing by late 2024. Substantial month-to-month swings were evident in 2025.

September 2026 brought a sharp rebound, with the quits rate rising to 5.0% from August’s 3.6%, an increase of 1.4 percentage points. That places the latest observation near the upper end of the pre-pandemic range, while remaining below the elevated rates seen during 2021–2022. The rebound interrupts the recent decline, but one month does not establish a sustained reversal.

The linear trend line remains nearly flat around 4.7% across the full period, masking substantial changes over time. The pandemic-era increase, subsequent decline, and latest rebound are more informative than the full-period slope alone. Recent observations have fallen both below and within the pre-pandemic range; these descriptive comparisons cannot establish whether the underlying baseline has changed.

Note: JOLTS data comes from a survey of business establishments and is subject to revision as additional responses are received and annual updates are applied.

COVID-19 Interpolation

Quit rates fell sharply during the initial pandemic disruption in early 2020. Because the goal of the subsequent analysis is to examine longer-term trends, remaining seasonality, and persistence, this abrupt movement could influence the patterns identified.

To reduce that influence, quit rate values from February through October 2020 were replaced using time-based linear interpolation between the January and November 2020 observations. This creates a smooth transition across those nine months. This interpolation is a separate modeling adjustment, not an additional seasonal adjustment to the BLS series.

This is a modeling choice that replaces actual observed variation with an assumed path through the disruption. It does not reconstruct what quit rates would have been without the pandemic, and its effect on forecast accuracy has not been evaluated against a model using the original observations.

The original BLS series is shown alongside the interpolated version above for transparency. All subsequent analysis uses the interpolated series, with observations outside the February–October 2020 window unchanged.

Seasonality

BLS publishes this quits-rate series on a seasonally adjusted basis, so this analysis explores remaining calendar-month patterns rather than the industry’s unadjusted seasonality. Monthly averages range from approximately 4.63% in February to 4.85% in June, a spread of about 0.22 percentage points. Relatively small differences are consistent with the prior seasonal adjustment and averaging across years, but these averages alone do not establish whether a pattern recurs consistently.

January through September averages include observations from 2016–2026, while October through December averages cover 2016–2025. This unequal coverage, along with the February–October 2020 interpolation, can influence the comparisons.

The annual overlay shows more pronounced differences in the overall level of quits than in a shared seasonal pattern. Rates in 2021 and 2022 were noticeably higher than those in 2016–2019. Several recent observations fell below the pre-pandemic range, followed by September 2026’s rebound to 5.0%. That rebound alone does not establish a recurring September effect.

The STL estimated seasonal component is generally small relative to the broader movements in the series, but its fluctuations become larger near the end of the sample. These estimates describe the interpolated, already seasonally adjusted series; they should not be interpreted as the industry’s original seasonal effects. The larger recent fluctuations also caution against describing remaining seasonality as uniformly weak.

Taken together, the charts do not show a clear, stable remaining annual pattern. Nonseasonal forecasting models are reasonable candidates, but these visuals alone cannot determine whether seasonal terms would improve forecast accuracy. That requires comparison on held-out observations.

Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation function shows how closely quit rates relate to their past values at different monthly lags. It is calculated using the interpolated series.

The ACF generally declines as the lag increases, remaining positive through lag 22, reaching approximately zero around lag 23, and becoming negative at longer lags. This pattern indicates substantial persistence, meaning that periods of relatively high or low quit rates tend to carry forward into subsequent months. Because the ACF is calculated on the level series, the gradual decline may reflect both serial dependence and longer-term movement rather than a specific autoregressive structure. Interpolation also smooths part of the series and can influence the measured persistence.

The dashed lines show approximate 95% white-noise reference bounds. They provide a visual benchmark for individual lags, rather than a definitive test across all lags or a method for selecting a forecasting model.

There is no distinct peak at lag 12 relative to neighboring lags. Such a peak can indicate an annual pattern, although its absence alone does not rule out seasonality. Since BLS already seasonally adjusts this series, the relevant question is whether annual dependence remains after that adjustment.

The ACF shows substantial persistence without a distinct annual peak, consistent with the earlier seasonality analysis. Nonseasonal forecasting models are therefore reasonable candidates, with their performance to be assessed through validation.

STL Decomposition

STL decomposition separates a time series into trend, seasonal, and residual components. The trend captures gradual changes in the level of the series. The seasonal component estimates recurring movements using a specified 12-month period. The residual contains the variation left after the estimated trend and seasonal components are removed.

The trend component captures the longer arc of the data. It shows relative stability through the pre-pandemic period, a smooth transition through the interpolated window, and a climb to an elevated period around 2021 and 2022. A broad decline follows, interrupted by a modest rise during 2025 before the trend turns downward again into September 2026. By the end of the sample, the estimated trend is below its pre-pandemic levels. September’s rebound in the input series has not produced a reversal in the estimated trend.

Because BLS already seasonally adjusts this series, the STL seasonal component represents remaining variation assigned to a recurring 12-month pattern by the decomposition. Its movements are generally small relative to the broader changes in the series, although they become larger near the end of the sample. This component alone does not establish that a stable annual pattern remains.

Residuals are generally smaller earlier in the sample, with larger positive and negative deviations appearing in 2025 and 2026. September 2026 has a sizable positive residual, indicating that much of its rebound is not captured by the estimated trend and seasonal components. These residuals describe variation left unexplained by the decomposition. They are not forecast errors.

The input panel reflects the interpolated series. The smooth transition from February through October 2020 results from replacing the original observations, rather than actual recorded quit activity. Trend and seasonal estimates near the end of the sample may also change as additional observations become available.

Forecasting

January through December 2025 was held back as a validation set. Four forecasting approaches were trained on January 2016 through December 2024 and used to forecast the following 12 months. The training series includes the February–October 2020 interpolation. The OLS model includes a linear time trend and month indicators. Their predictions were compared against observed 2025 values using mean absolute error, measured in percentage points. Several nonseasonal ARIMA specifications were compared, with ARIMA(0,1,1) producing the lowest validation error.

ARIMA(0,1,1) produced the lowest MAE at 0.637 percentage points, compared with 0.725 percentage points for the Naive model. This represents approximately a 12.1% reduction in MAE based on the rounded results. The Naive model simply carries forward the final training observation. Its second-place finish shows that this simple approach provided a competitive benchmark in the 2025 validation period.

Because the 2025 results were used to select the ARIMA specification, they represent model-selection performance rather than an independent final test of the selected model. The ranking reflects performance over this particular year and does not establish that ARIMA will perform best in every forecasting period.

None of the evaluated models captured the timing and magnitude of the 2025 swings. Quit rates rose to 5.5% in June before falling sharply later in the year. ARIMA and Naive produced flat forecasts, ETS projected a decline, and OLS remained above most observed values. ARIMA achieved the lowest average error without reproducing the year's month-to-month movements.

ARIMA(0,1,1) was then retrained on the interpolated series through December 2025 to generate a January–December 2026 forecast with 80% and 95% prediction intervals. The model was not updated with observed 2026 values. Its point forecast is flat, a feature of this specification without a drift term. This forecast uses the current historical data, which may include BLS revisions, rather than necessarily reproducing the forecast that would have been available in December 2025.

Actual quit rates declined from 4.7% in January to 3.6% in August, with some intervening increases, before rebounding to 5.0% in September. September's increase of 1.4 percentage points interrupts the recent decline and changes the assessment from one focused solely on falling rates to one that must also account for a sharp rebound. A flat forecast cannot reproduce these month-to-month movements, although its average error can still be compared with alternative models.

The prediction intervals describe uncertainty under the fitted model and its assumptions. An observation falling within an interval does not, by itself, demonstrate accurate forecasting. Evaluation should consider the size and direction of forecast errors across the available 2026 months, alongside interval coverage. Additional observations will help assess whether September's rebound persists and how the fixed forecast performs over the remainder of the year.

Key Findings

  • September’s quits rate rebounded to 5.0% from 3.6% in August. One month does not establish a sustained reversal.
  • Monthly averages are similar in this already seasonally adjusted series. Recent STL fluctuations warrant caution when describing remaining seasonality.
  • ARIMA(0,1,1) had the lowest 2025 validation MAE, approximately 12.1% below the naive baseline based on rounded results. This is model-selection performance.
  • The fixed 2026 forecast remains flat near 4.53%. It does not reproduce the decline through August or September’s rebound.
  • Interpolation, historical revisions, and model assumptions limit how the findings and forecast intervals should be interpreted.
