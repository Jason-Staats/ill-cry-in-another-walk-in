I'll Cry in Another Walk-In

A Time Series Analysis of Quit Rates in Accommodation and Food Services

Jason Staats

Updated June 2026; data through May 2026

Overview

The accommodation and food services sector is widely associated with elevated employee turnover. This report examines monthly quit rates from January 2016 through May 2026 using the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey.

The central question is whether the post-pandemic surge in voluntary separations has fully unwound, or whether elevated turnover remains a structural feature of the industry. This analysis serves as a companion to Up or On the Rocks?, which looks at employment levels in the same sector.

ProviderU.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS)
PeriodJanuary 2016 - May 2026
FrequencyMonthly
UnitsQuits rate, percentage of employment

The JOLTS quits rate measures voluntary separations as a percentage of employment in the reference period. It captures worker-initiated exits and is distinct from layoffs, discharges, retirements, or other employer-initiated separations.

Methodology

The notebook follows a structured time-series workflow: full series visualization with a linear trend overlay, seasonality analysis, autocorrelation analysis, STL decomposition, and multi-model forecasting with holdout validation.

Quit-rate values from February 2020 through October 2020 were replaced with linearly interpolated estimates during decomposition and forecasting. This treats the early pandemic decline in quit activity as an external mobility shock rather than a durable change in the underlying quits-rate process.

Validation design. Four models were trained on January 2016 through December 2024 and validated against January through December 2025. Model performance was assessed using Mean Absolute Error in percentage points.

Full Series

Quit rates held relatively steady between 2016 and early 2020, averaging around 4.5%. After a sharp but brief pandemic-era decline, rates climbed from mid-2021 through early 2022 to a sustained peak near 6%, then trended downward toward earlier levels by late 2024.

The linear trend line reflects a mild upward drift across the full period. Even after the decline from the 2021-2022 peak, the series ends slightly above where it began, suggesting some shift in worker behavior or bargaining power relative to 2016 norms.

COVID-19 Interpolation

Quit rates in early 2020 deviated sharply from the rest of the series. Interpolation bridges the gap between the pre-pandemic level and the return to a more typical range, preventing that isolated period from dominating the trend and seasonal estimates.

Seasonality

Unlike employment in the sector, quits rates do not show a strong recurring seasonal pattern. Monthly averages are relatively flat, and the year-over-year overlay is driven more by level shifts over time than by a stable calendar rhythm.

The STL seasonal component reinforces this point: most swings remain small, and the direction of the seasonal effect is not stable enough to justify a seasonal forecasting structure.

Autocorrelation

The autocorrelation function declines gradually from lag 1 onward, remaining positive for roughly the first 15 to 18 lags before crossing zero. That smooth decay is characteristic of an autoregressive process where recent values matter, but their influence fades over time.

What is absent is a pronounced lag-12 spike. That supports the seasonality finding and explains why the final forecast relies on a non-seasonal ARIMA specification rather than SARIMA.

STL Decomposition

STL decomposition separates the adjusted series into observed, trend, seasonal, and residual components. The trend shows stability before the pandemic, a climb to the 2021-2022 peak, and a gradual decline back toward earlier levels from 2023 onward.

The muted seasonal component is the key takeaway. Most values stay close to zero, while the residuals show more pronounced deviations around the unusual volatility of 2025.

Forecasting

To evaluate out-of-sample performance, the models forecast January through December 2025 after training on January 2016 through December 2024. ARIMA(1,1,1) produced the lowest average error and was selected for the 2026 forecast.

No model anticipated the volatility that 2025 produced, including the June spike to 5.5% and the later sharp pullback. That result reflects the limits of models trained on a relatively low and stable end-of-2024 level rather than a simple modeling failure.

ARIMA was retrained on the full series through December 2025 and used to generate a 2026 forward forecast with 80% and 95% confidence intervals. The point forecast is relatively flat, while January through May 2026 actuals stayed within the 80% confidence interval despite month-to-month volatility.

Key Findings

  • Quit rates averaged approximately 4.5% from 2016 through early 2020, reflecting an already elevated turnover baseline.
  • The pandemic sharply suppressed quit activity in early 2020, coinciding with labor market uncertainty and reduced worker mobility.
  • Quit rates rose sharply between mid-2021 and early 2022, reaching a sustained peak near 6%.
  • Rates declined from 2022 through 2024, approaching pre-pandemic levels by late 2024.
  • The series has negligible seasonal structure, in contrast to the strong summer employment peaks seen in the companion employment analysis.
  • January through May 2026 quit rates ranged from 4.0% to 4.7%, all within the ARIMA 80% confidence interval.
